The <span class="lagoon">seven colors</span> of the lagoon are real, and there's a concrete geological reason for them. Bacalar sits on a limestone bed that water has dissolved over thousands of years, creating depths that change abruptly: shallow zones with bright sandy bottoms, deep cenotes where the floor disappears into dark blue, and shoals covered in microorganisms that tint the water pale green. When the sun hits that topography, the tones split into bands of turquoise, emerald, cobalt, and near-black.

There is no coral here. Bacalar Lagoon is freshwater, fed by cenotes and underground springs. What looks like reef — those gray and brown mounds in shallow water — is something far older and far rarer.

## Stromatolites: the oldest living things on Earth

Stromatolites are colonies of cyanobacteria that grow in stacked layers. Each layer is a generation: the bacteria trap sediment, cement it, die, and the next layer grows on top. The process is extraordinarily slow — a mound the size of a coffee table can take **centuries or millennia** to form.

These organisms have been doing the same thing for <span class="clay">3.5 billion years</span>. They are, literally, the reason Earth's atmosphere has oxygen: during the planet's Great Oxygenation Event, around 2.4 billion years ago, stromatolites were the producers. Without them, you wouldn't be reading this.

{{quote:Anonymous|Living stromatolites are extremely rare. They survive only in a handful of unusually saline or chemically distinctive places — and here, in Bacalar, one of the largest freshwater stromatolite reefs in the world.}}

The least invasive way to see them is from a sailboat: no engine, no wake, no noise that stresses the colonies. It's also the best way to grasp the scale — a single bed of stromatolites can stretch hundreds of meters underwater.

## Where to see them

The main stromatolite beds are in the southern half of the lagoon:

- **Cocalitos** — the most visited spot. Beds are visible at about 1.5 meters of depth.
- **Los Rápidos** — where the lagoon narrows and the current picks up. Extensive stromatolite formations on both sides of the channel.
- **Canal de los Piratas** — scattered beds along the shoreline.

## Rules you'll be asked to follow

Stromatolites are fragile. The living surface layer is **only a few millimeters** thick, and contact breaks it. So:

1. **Don't touch them, don't stand on them.** A single misplaced step destroys decades of growth.
2. **Don't apply sunscreen before entering the water.** Even "biodegradable" formulas alter the lagoon's chemistry. The local rule is sunscreen 30 minutes before leaving your hotel — so your skin has absorbed it — or UPF 50+ rashguards.
3. **Boats anchor only in deep water.** Your captain knows; if they drop anchor near a shallow bed, you can ask them to move.
4. **Swim calmly.** Hard kicks stir up sediment that settles on the colonies and smothers them.

These aren't tourist-paperwork rules. They're the only thing keeping Bacalar's stromatolites from following the trajectory of the Bahamas, where mass-tourism damage is already irreversible across whole zones.

## When to go

The best time for stromatolites is **early morning**, ideally before 9 a.m. Three reasons:

- The water is calmer — no wind chop or boat wake.
- There's less boat traffic.
- The low sun lights the bottom at an angle, making the colony textures visible.

For exactly this, the [sunrise paddle / kayak tour](/tour/paddle-and-or-kayak-tour-at-sunrise) is almost ideal: early start, silent kayak, small group. If you want something more atmospheric, there's the [underglow sunrise paddle](/tour/laguna-lumina-sunrise-paddle-uderglow-experience) version.

Dry season (November through April) gives the best visibility. In the rainy months (June–October), heavy rain can cloud the southern zone for a couple of days after a downpour, but the upside is cooler weather and lower prices.

## Picking a tour that's actually worth it

Tours that cross the lagoon vary widely. To actually see stromatolites, look for:

- **It passes through Cocalitos or Los Rápidos.** Ask explicitly.
- **A small group.** A sailboat with 6–8 people, or a kayak, is ideal. A 30-passenger catamaran with loud music is not.
- **A local, informed captain.** The good ones know where the beds are, stop to explain what you're seeing, and make sure the group respects the rules.
